\bigbreak
Humans cooperate at a scale that remains puzzling, and economists and behavioural scientists have long pointed to reputation as a central explanation \cite{wu_reputation_2016, przepiorka_order_2017} [Wu, J., Balliet, D. \& Van Lang, other refs from Frey 11-17]. Reputation allows indirect reciprocity [Alexander, 1987; Nowak and Sigmund, 1998] guides people into choosing whether and with whom to cooperate [REF; REF], and, because a good reputation pays, it in turn rewards cooperative behaviour. This literature has convincingly shown why reputation ``works'': it reveals cooperative dispositions, benefits its holders, and thereby stabilises cooperation.
\bigbreak
This literature, however, rests on two assumptions that are rarely questioned. First, reputation is assumed to be accurate [REF Milinski], or at least a noisy but unbiased proxy for behaviour [Julien Proc B, REF Hilbe 2018 noisy]. This often holds by design: in canonical models [Nowak \& Sigmund] and experiments [Milinski Nature and Science], ``reputation'' is simply an image score, a record of past play REF Wedekind. When accuracy is tested at all [ref four puzzles], the focus is on how far reputations agree with behaviour, not on how they systematically depart from it. Second, reputation is usually treated as an independent variable whose consequences are studied, rather than as an outcome. Models need reputations to vary within a population, as a reflection of intrinsic variation in altruistic tendencies, since otherwise there would be nothing to track, but the distribution of reputations has received little attention.
\bigbreak
Sociologists take a radically different view. For them, reputation is a form of status, ``a dimension of inequality with its own sustaining social dynamic'' [AJS 2013, p. 4; REF Weber]. They have accordingly asked how reputation is socially constructed [REF Lynn]: how it becomes persistent [REF Tilly], self-perpetuating [REF Ridgeway], and arbitrary [REF Frey]. The typical story is that some actors first gain an edge, through chance or greater resources, and some process then locks it in. Merton called this the Matthew effect [REF Merton], or ``cumulative advantage", meaning that an advantage today produces a larger advantage tomorrow [REF DiPrete]. Such ``success breeds success'' dynamics [REF van de Rijt] have been documented in many domains [REF Merton, van de Rijt, Salganik].
\bigbreak
The main mechanism proposed for reputational cumulative advantage is \textit{status advantage}: the same signal is read differently depending on who sends it. In Ridgeway's words, ``the same idea `sounds better' coming from the advantaged than the disadvantaged'' [Ridgeway address]. Status advantage is often treated as equivalent to a Matthew effect (Merton treats them as equivalent), but the two are distinct: the former is a biased signal reception, the latter a self-amplifying dynamic of inequality. Formal models show that status advantage can amplify reputational inequality without decoupling reputation from underlying quality, which Lynn [REF] calls \textit{weak} (as opposed to \textit{strong}) social construction. Whether status advantage is empirically strong enough produces a genuine Matthew effect is also an open question.

In this paper, we analyse the dynamics of reputation under the light of this sociological insight. In eight field sites, we measured reputation for `fairness' and `generosity', both before and after a dictator game  - a simple economic game that is seen as a measure of people's generosity and prosociality. We also asked respondents to rate the decisions themselves, to assess how their behaviour was judged, independently from the reputation dynamics. Reputation should be particularly salient in these communities: they are small and tight-knit, people interact with one another on a day-to-day basis and are often reliant on one another for their livelihoods. 


This design addresses two gaps. For research on cooperation, it tests whether reputation simply mirrors behaviour or is a self-sustaining dimension of inequality [Ridgeway]. For sociology, it extends the status perspective to a new and arguably more typical domain. Most sociological evidence comes from settings such as markets, science, and celebrity, where reputations are extremely skewed, quality is highly uncertain, and audiences cannot know everyone. There, reputation dynamics probably resemble capital accumulation more than belief updating. Apple, Taylor Swift and Daron Acemoglu clearly benefit from status advantage, but does a generous Jane Doe? Sociologists have claimed that the effect was a more general mechanism for status inequality: Di Prete \& Eirch REF propose it as ``a general mechanism for inequality across any temporal process" (p. 271), while Merton also mentioned that his Matthew effect should apply not only to academia but also to ``other domains of social life" (p. 606). 

The Expectation States Framework school (REF Berger REF Ridgeway) used analogous ideas to explain the persistence of `status beliefs', i.e., beliefs that some groups are more socially worthy or competent than others.

\section{Model}
At time 1, an agent has a reputation $r_1 \in \mathbb{R}$, meant to track her (secret) level of generosity $g$. She sends a signal $s \in \mathbb{R}$, which feeds into his reputation by the following equation:
$$r_2 = (1-\lambda) r_1 + \lambda s
$$

If $s=g$, or a noisy signal centered on $g$, then clearly, after a few rounds of such updates, $r$ should converge at or around $s$.

Now assume that there is status advantage: signal reception is biased by existing reputation. Formally, if the agent's actual generosity is $g$, the received signal is $g + \beta s$, with $\beta$ the weight of status advantage. 

In other words,
$$
r_2 = (1-\lambda) r_1 + \lambda(g+\beta r_1) = (1-\lambda(1 - \beta))r_1 + \lambda g
$$
